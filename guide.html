Screenplay Format: A Student Guide

How film and TV scripts are laid out, and why. Written for creative writing, English and film studies classes.

Why screenplays use a strict format

A screenplay is a blueprint for a film, not a finished work of literature. Directors, actors, set designers and producers all read the same pages, so the format is standardized. In 12-point Courier with standard margins, one page works out to about one minute of screen time. That lets a production team estimate a film's length just by counting pages.

The six elements

1. Scene heading (slugline). This tells the reader where and when a scene happens. It begins with INT. (interior) or EXT. (exterior), then the location, then the time of day, all in capitals.

INT. SCHOOL LIBRARY - DAY

2. Action. This describes what the audience sees and hears, in present tense. Keep paragraphs short, usually four lines or fewer. Write only what could appear on screen, not characters' private thoughts. Capitalize a character's name the first time they appear.

MAYA (16) slides a battered notebook across the table. Rain streaks the window behind her.

3. Character cue. This is the name of the person speaking, in capitals and indented toward the center of the page. Extensions such as (V.O.) for voice-over and (O.S.) for off-screen go after the name.

4. Dialogue. This is the words spoken, placed directly under the character cue in a narrower column.

5. Parenthetical. This is a short direction for how a line is delivered, in parentheses between the cue and the dialogue. Use these sparingly, because actors and directors interpret the delivery.

                    MAYA
          (whispering)
     You have to read the last page.

6. Transition. This is an editing instruction, aligned to the right, such as CUT TO: or FADE OUT. Modern scripts use them rarely.

Page layout

  • Font: 12-point Courier or Courier Prime.
  • Margins: 1.5 inches on the left (for binding), about 1 inch on the right, top and bottom.
  • Dialogue starts about 2.5 inches from the left edge, and character cues about 3.7 inches.
  • Page numbers go in the top right corner. The title page has no number.

Common mistakes

  • Writing camera directions ("we zoom in") when describing the action would be enough.
  • Long blocks of action that slow the read.
  • Describing feelings the audience can't see. Show them through behavior instead.
  • Too many parentheticals.

The Fountain plain-text format

Fountain is an open standard for writing screenplays as plain text. A line in capitals that starts with INT. or EXT. becomes a scene heading. A capitalized name followed by a line of text becomes a character cue and dialogue. Lines in parentheses become parentheticals. Because it's just text, a Fountain script opens in any editor and lasts for decades. Quill imports and exports Fountain.

Practice exercise

Write a one-page scene (about one minute of screen time) with two characters who want different things. Use one scene heading, at least three action paragraphs and one parenthetical. Then read it aloud and time it. Is it close to a minute?
